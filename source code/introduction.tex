\section{Introduction}


Since the first computers, the trend has been to miniaturise these machines in order to increase their efficiency and convenience. But there are clear physical limits to miniaturisation. Benioff \cite{cps80} proposed that the ultimate limit of miniaturisation would be a quantum mechanical system. Soon afterwards, Deutsch \cite{qtct85} showed that the benefits of a quantum computer were not merely its size and energy savings: an entirely new mode of computation became possible, exploiting entangled quantum particles and quantum parallelism. Following this, Shor \cite{ptafpf94} discovered a polynomial-time prime factorisation algorithm for a quantum computer, generating great interest in the field. Together with Grover's algorithm for fast searching of an unsorted database \cite{fqma96}, Shor's algorithm provided the motivation for scientists to investigate the realisation of physical quantum computing devices.

Despite the rapid progress in the theory of quantum computation, experimental implementation of a quantum computer faces major challenges. Although very basic quantum computing devices have been demonstrated using a variety of techniques, the realisation of a useful, large-scale quantum computer in the near future remains uncertain.

For this reason, quantum-computer simulation on classical computers has become a popular topic. Several libraries\footnote{libquantum (\emph{http://www.enyo.de/libquantum/})} and standalone applications have been developed for this purpose, some free,\footnote{jaQuzzi (\emph{http://www.eng.buffalo.edu/\~{}phygons/jaQuzzi/jaQuzzi.html})}\footnote{Qudit (\emph{http://www.compsoc.nuigalway.ie/\~{}damo642/QuantumSimulator/})} some proprietary,\footnote{Senko's simulator (\emph{http://www.senko-corp.co.jp/qcs/qcp/qcpe.html})} and several dedicated languages have appeared.\footnote{QCL (\emph{http://tph.tuwien.ac.at/\~{}oemer/qcl.html})}\footnote{Q language (\emph{http://sra.itc.it/people/serafini/qlang/})} Because the computational cost of state-vector simulation grows exponentially with the number of qubits, all of these tools eventually hit the same scaling wall. To push this wall back further, this thesis discusses a method for quantum-computer simulation on a distributed cluster. A similar approach has been pursued by Glendinning and \"Omer \cite{pgsq03}.
