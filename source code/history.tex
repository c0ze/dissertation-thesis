\section{History of Quantum Computation}


At the turn of the 1980s, working independently, Paul Benioff and Richard Feynman each claimed that quantum computing could be possible. Simulating quantum systems on classical computers requires an exponentially large amount of time, and Feynman's (1982) starting point was exactly this difficulty: he proposed that a quantum system could simulate other quantum systems more efficiently \cite{spwc82}. Benioff (1980) was motivated rather by continuing the trend of miniaturisation in the computer industry \cite{cps80}: as circuits continue to shrink they will eventually reach the atomic level, where quantum mechanical effects become a major limiting factor, so the only feasible long-term solution was to construct a computer that would make use of those effects rather than fight them.

In 1985 David Deutsch published a paper \cite{qtct85} which proposed a quantum Turing machine, which made use of a before unnoticed property of quantum computers. Though severely underrated at its time, Deutsch's paper would later be considered a landmark in quantum computation theory. In his paper, Deutsch showed that it is possible to perform some tasks on quantum computers much more efficiently than classical computers. Deutsch's idea was to make use of a phenomenon which is known as quantum superposition. An isolated electron, if placed in a superposition, can travel along many different paths through a circuit. If the memory could be placed in a quantum superposition before applying the algorithm, it could be in multiple states simultaneously. So just one run-through the algorithm would calculate all computational paths simultaneously. This idea (Which was to be called \emph{quantum parallelism}) would be the foundation of all quantum algorithms.

The examples in Deutsch's paper were of limited use and due to their probabilistic nature, should be run several times to ensure the correct outcome. But these early hints in the field of quantum computation theory stirred the imaginations of many scientists, and about 10 years later Peter Shor of AT\&T labs came up with a factorization algorithm for quantum computers.

In 1994 Peter Shor of AT\&T developed a quantum algorithm for integer factorisation \cite{ptafpf94} that runs in time polynomial in the number of input bits --- super-polynomially faster than any known classical algorithm. This discovery triggered enormous interest in quantum computation, because factorisation is widely believed to be classically hard: the best known classical algorithms (the general number field sieve and its variants) run in sub-exponential but super-polynomial time. The decision version of factoring lies in $\mathrm{NP} \cap \mathrm{co\text{-}NP}$, which is one reason most complexity theorists do \emph{not} expect it to be $\mathrm{NP}$-complete --- such a result would collapse $\mathrm{NP} = \mathrm{co\text{-}NP}$. Whether factoring is in $\mathrm{P}$ classically remains an open problem. These difficulties underpin many modern cryptosystems, most prominently RSA (the Rivest--Shamir--Adleman protocol), which rests on the difficulty of factoring a very large number that is the product of two prime secret keys. Shor's algorithm shows that quantum computers can factor such numbers in polynomial time, which would compromise the security of RSA and other widely deployed key-exchange protocols once large enough quantum hardware exists.

Only two years later Lov Grover of Bell Labs invented a quantum algorithm for fast searching of an unsorted database \cite{fqma96}. Although Grover's algorithm gives only a quadratic speed-up over the best classical algorithm --- in contrast to Shor's super-polynomial separation --- searching is so foundational a primitive, and Grover's algorithm so readily plugs in to amplify the efficiency of many existing algorithms, that its discovery carries comparable importance.

These algorithms show the capabilities of a quantum computer, and the main motivation behind the research into quantum computation. It is logical to expect even more powerful algorithms to be discovered in the near future. However, an implementation of a quantum computer which would be capable of convenient applications seems far away. At the time of the original 2004 submission, quantum computers could operate only on a few qubits, and under extreme environmental conditions; the present state of the art is summarised in the next chapter.
