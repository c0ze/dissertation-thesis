\section{Mathematical Basics}

\subsection{Dirac notation and the single-qubit state space}

Qubits are conventionally represented as \emph{ket} vectors in the Dirac (bra--ket) notation, which is standard in quantum physics. A ket $\ket{\psi}$ denotes a column vector and its conjugate transpose $\bra{\psi}$ is the bra (a row vector). In quantum computation, the expression $\ket{0}$ represents the quantum analogue of the classical bit value $0$, and $\ket{1}$ the analogue of $1$.

The state of a qubit can then be written as
\begin{equation}
\ket{\psi} = \alpha\ket{0} + \beta\ket{1},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}$ are the \emph{probability amplitudes} of $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$ respectively, and (by Postulate 1) $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$. The probability of obtaining outcome $0$ on a computational-basis measurement is $|\alpha|^2$, and of $1$ is $|\beta|^2$. More generally, an $n$-qubit register has $2^n$ amplitudes whose squared magnitudes sum to $1$.

In matrix form the computational-basis states are
\begin{equation}
\ket{0} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\ket{1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
and an arbitrary single-qubit state is the unit vector $(\alpha,\beta)^T$.

\subsection{The tensor product and the multi-qubit state space}

The state space of a composite system is the tensor product of the component state spaces (Postulate 4). For matrices $A$ ($n\times m$) and $B$ ($p\times q$), the Kronecker (tensor) product is the $np\times mq$ matrix
\begin{equation}
A\otimes B \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} a_{11}B & \cdots & a_{1m}B \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{n1}B & \cdots & a_{nm}B \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Specialised to two column vectors $\vec{a}\in\mathbb{C}^n$ and $\vec{b}\in\mathbb{C}^m$,
\begin{equation}
\vec{a}\otimes\vec{b} \;=\; \bigl(a_1 b_1,\; a_1 b_2,\; \ldots,\; a_1 b_m,\; a_2 b_1,\; \ldots,\; a_2 b_m,\; \ldots,\; a_n b_1,\; \ldots,\; a_n b_m\bigr)^T.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Convention.} Throughout this thesis we number the qubits of an $n$-qubit register $0, 1, \ldots, n-1$ from the least significant bit upward --- the natural indexing for code --- and write tensor products in \emph{big-endian} order, with the more significant qubit on the left. So for two qubits indexed $1$ (more significant) and $0$ (less significant), with single-qubit kets $\ket{a}=(a_0, a_1)^T$ on qubit $1$ and $\ket{b}=(b_0, b_1)^T$ on qubit $0$,
\begin{equation}
\ket{a}_1 \otimes \ket{b}_0 \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} a_0 b_0 \\ a_0 b_1 \\ a_1 b_0 \\ a_1 b_1 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the index of basis state $\ket{x_{n-1} x_{n-2} \ldots x_0}$ in the amplitude vector is the integer $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} x_k \cdot 2^k$. Fixing this convention once and using it consistently is essential: changing endianness mid-derivation swaps amplitude indices and silently breaks every matrix--vector multiplication that follows.

\subsection{Single-qubit gates as operators on a register}

If $U$ is a $2\times 2$ unitary and we want to apply it to qubit $k$ of an $n$-qubit register (with qubits indexed $0,\ldots,n-1$ from the least significant bit, which is the convenient indexing for code), the operator on the full $2^n$-dimensional space is
\begin{equation}
U_k \;=\; I^{\otimes(n-1-k)} \,\otimes\, U \,\otimes\, I^{\otimes k}.
\label{eq:single-qubit-on-register}
\end{equation}
This is a $2^n \times 2^n$ matrix, but it is extremely sparse: each row has exactly two non-zero entries, in two specific columns. We will see in Section~\ref{sec:parallel} that materialising $U_k$ as a dense matrix is the single mistake one most wants to avoid in a state-vector simulator --- the matrix never needs to be built explicitly.

\subsection{Unitary representation of arbitrary classical functions}

A quantum program transforming an $n$-qubit register from input to output state can in principle be written as a $2^n \times 2^n$ unitary matrix which, when multiplied by the input-state vector, yields the output-state vector. Quantum computation must be information-lossless (and therefore reversible at each gate); the operator must be unitary.

What about classical functions that are not bijections? Any classical function $f:\{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}^m$ can be made reversible --- and hence unitary --- using the Bennett construction. Append an $m$-qubit ancilla register and define $U_f$ on the entire joint Hilbert space by its action on computational basis states,
\begin{equation}
U_f\, \ket{x}\ket{y} \;=\; \ket{x}\bigl|\,y \oplus f(x)\bigr\rangle,
\label{eq:bennett-xor}
\end{equation}
where $\oplus$ denotes bitwise XOR. The first register is preserved and the second is shifted by $f(x)$, so $U_f$ is a permutation of computational basis states and therefore a unitary matrix on the joint $2^{n+m}$-dimensional space \cite{bennett1973}. The familiar special case
\begin{equation*}
U_f\, \ket{x}\ket{0} \;=\; \ket{x}\bigl|\,f(x)\bigr\rangle
\end{equation*}
recovers the value of $f(x)$ in the ancilla register, but the full XOR form is needed to make $U_f$ unitary on \emph{all} of $\mathbb{C}^{2^{n+m}}$, not just on the subspace where the ancilla is $\ket{0}$. This is the standard ``bit oracle'' pattern used by Deutsch--Jozsa, Grover, and Shor (and reappears in Sections~\ref{sec:grover} and~\ref{sec:shor}). The earlier claim --- common in informal expositions --- that a unitary exists ``whenever $f$ is one-to-one'' is technically insufficient: it is the Bennett ancilla trick that promotes \emph{any} classical function to a unitary, regardless of whether the original $f$ was injective.

The combined effect of single-qubit gates applied in parallel to disjoint qubits in a register is the tensor product of the individual gate matrices. Combined with the in-place sparse-application algorithm of Section~\ref{sec:parallel}, this is all the machinery one needs to simulate any quantum circuit.
