\section{Quantum Computation}

We are concerned with using quantum computation to produce solutions to problems considered intractable for classical computers.\footnote{An \emph{intractable} problem is one for which execution time grows exponentially as input size increases linearly.} To see how quantum computers can solve classically intractable problems, we need to examine how they perform computation.

The building block of the quantum computer is the \emph{qubit}, or quantum bit. A qubit is an abstract value, realised by nano-scale properties of physical matter. Qubits have been implemented using ion charge, photon polarisation, nuclear spins of atoms, and nuclear spins of impurities in silicon chips.

Classical bits abstractly represent values of $0$ or $1$ by the absence or presence of voltage. A qubit, by superposition, represents a linear combination of $0$ and $1$ simultaneously.

An $n$-qubit register holds a superposition over $2^n$ basis states. Two qubits, for example, hold a superposition over the four states $\ket{00},\ket{01},\ket{10},\ket{11}$, each weighted by a complex \emph{probability amplitude}. Probability amplitudes are mathematically analogous to a wave: they can be positive or negative (or in general complex), and they interfere. The classical probability of an outcome is the squared magnitude of its amplitude. Quantum computation manipulates probability amplitudes through unitary gates so that, on measurement, the amplitudes of desired outcomes interfere constructively while those of undesired outcomes interfere destructively.

\subsection{Unitary evolution as computation}

A single qubit lives in the Hilbert space $\mathbb{C}^2$; an $n$-qubit register lives in the tensor product
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{H} \;=\; \underbrace{\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \cdots \otimes \mathbb{C}^2}_{n \text{ factors}} \;=\; \mathbb{C}^{2^n}.
\end{equation}
We compute a function $f$ on these qubits by applying a unitary operator $U$ to the state vector:
\begin{equation}
\ket{\psi'} \;=\; U \ket{\psi}.
\end{equation}
Any classical function can be expressed in this form using the Bennett ancilla construction from the previous section. We can therefore think of $U$ as a basic quantum gate, performing the role of a classical truth table, and represent it as a unitary matrix --- which by definition is reversible, since $U^{-1} = U^\dagger$.

\subsection{From Hamiltonians to gates}

To apply a target unitary $U$ on physical hardware, we find a Hamiltonian $H$ which solves
\begin{equation}
U \;=\; e^{-iHt/\hbar}.
\label{eq:U-from-H}
\end{equation}
A solution exists as long as $U$ is unitary: by the spectral theorem, every unitary matrix can be written as $e^{iA}$ for some Hermitian $A$, so taking $H = -\hbar A / t$ gives the desired Hamiltonian. From the standpoint of algorithms we can usually treat $H$ as an implementation detail and reason directly with $U$.

By applying $U$ to a superposition over all $2^n$ inputs, we effectively apply $f$ to all of them in parallel --- the so-called \emph{quantum parallelism}. This parallelism is the basic resource that quantum algorithms exploit, though as discussed below, exponential parallelism does not directly translate into exponential computational power; algorithms must arrange interference so that useful information survives measurement.

\subsection{Measurement and the central challenge}

Obtaining the computed answer presents a new challenge. The superposition of $n$ qubits ranges over $2^n$ possible computational-basis states; we sometimes refer to it loosely as a ``wave function''. Measuring this state (Postulate 3) collapses the superposition, selecting one basis state from the $2^n$ with probability equal to the squared magnitude of its amplitude. This selection effectively destroys the rest of the computed information. Quantum algorithms must therefore use the interference property of amplitudes to concentrate probability mass onto basis states encoding the correct answer before measurement is performed.

\subsection{Practical limitations}

Quantum computing is not without drawbacks. The exponential parallelism inherent in quantum computation does not, by itself, imply exponential computational power. Practical implementation is hindered by decoherence, caused both by interaction with the environment and by intrinsic decay of quantum states. Quantum error-correcting codes are necessary to mitigate decoherence and error propagation in the real world; these codes require many physical qubits to encode a single logical qubit. Some quantum architectures have impractical environmental requirements (e.g., operating at temperatures near absolute zero), with classical control hardware that does not function well in the same environment.

Because of these difficulties, simulation on classical computers remains essential to algorithm development and analysis. Quantum simulators allow inspection of the full state, accumulation of errors under noise models, and feasibility studies that would otherwise require physical hardware. However, simulating an $n$-qubit register on a classical machine requires storing $2^n$ complex amplitudes, which restricts simulators to relatively small register sizes --- the central scaling problem that motivates the parallel implementation discussed later in this thesis.

When this thesis was first submitted in 2004, real quantum computers operated on roughly 5--7 qubits; Shor's algorithm had been demonstrated on a 7-qubit nuclear-magnetic-resonance machine in 2001 \cite{vandersypen2001}. Two decades on, the picture is very different: superconducting processors with hundreds of physical qubits are now in service.\footnote{For up-to-date device counts see IBM's processor catalogue (\emph{https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/docs/en/guides/processor-types}); as of this revision the Heron r2 carries 156 qubits and the Nighthawk r1 carries 120.} Even so, error correction is still a heavy multiplier on physical-to-logical qubit counts, and classical simulation remains essential for algorithm design and verification --- which is the practical motivation for the work that follows.
